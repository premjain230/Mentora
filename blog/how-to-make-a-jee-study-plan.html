JEE Study Plan: How to Make One (with a Timetable)

A Learning Allied · Updated August 13, 2026 ·
Short answer A JEE study plan is a realistic daily and weekly schedule that breaks the JEE syllabus into topics, allocates time to Physics, Chemistry and Mathematics, and reserves fixed slots for practice problems, mock tests and revision. To make one, audit your syllabus, set a target score, divide topics by weightage, build a weekly timetable, and review it every Sunday.

Most JEE aspirants don't lack hard work — they lack a plan that survives a busy school week. This guide walks you through building a JEE study plan you can actually follow, with a sample timetable you can adapt to your own schedule.

What makes a JEE study plan work

A study plan works when it meets three tests. First, it fits your real week — school timings, travel, tuition and sleep are already on the calendar, so study blocks fit around them. Second, it is specific enough that at any moment you know what to study next, instead of deciding on the spot. Third, it includes built-in revision and mock tests, because JEE is a problem-solving exam and you improve fastest by practising under exam-like conditions.

The plan below follows these three rules. It doesn't ask for superhuman discipline — it asks for a repeatable routine you can run on an ordinary school day.

Know the exam before you plan

JEE Main (Paper 1 for B.E./B.Tech) is an online, multiple-choice plus numerical-answer test of three subjects. As of the current pattern, each subject carries 25 questions — 20 multiple-choice questions (MCQs) and 5 numerical-value questions — and each question is worth 4 marks. MCQs carry a negative mark of −1 for a wrong answer; numerical-value questions carry no negative marking. Always confirm the latest pattern on the official NTA website before the exam.

Three things about this pattern shape your plan:

  • Speed and accuracy matter. You don't have time to re-derive everything, so daily practice of timed questions is non-negotiable.
  • Numerical questions are high-value. They have no negative marking, so a correct attempt is a pure gain. Practising them should be a permanent part of your week.
  • All three subjects need equal attention. The marks are split evenly, so dropping a subject drops your score fast.
Where to check official details

Syllabus, exam pattern and dates: jeemain.nta.nic.in · Chapter-wise NCERT syllabus: ncert.nic.in. Use these as your source of truth rather than coaching-class rumour.

How to make your JEE study plan, step by step

1

Audit your syllabus and deadline

Download the official syllabus and mark each chapter as done, partially done, or not started. Count the not-started chapters and divide by the weeks you have. This gives you a rough weekly target — for example, 40 remaining chapters over 20 weeks means about two chapters a week. This one calculation makes the whole plan realistic.

2

Set a target and be honest about your gaps

Set a target based on your mock-test history, not a round number like "99 percentile". If you currently score 70 in a subject, a realistic near-term target is 90, not 100. Write down your two weakest chapters in each subject — your plan should spend the first few weeks on exactly those.

3

Divide the syllabus into weekly chunks

For every week, pick a small set of new topics (one to two chapters) and a set of practice targets. Keep the split roughly even across Physics, Chemistry and Mathematics. Rotate subjects across days so each one gets at least 90 minutes every day or alternate day.

4

Build a weekly timetable, not just a to-do list

A to-do list tells you what; a timetable tells you when. Map your study blocks onto your actual week, one row per day. The sample in the next section shows the shape. The key is that a fixed time for each subject removes daily decision-making, which is where most plans fall apart.

5

Reserve non-negotiable slots: practice, revision, mocks

Every day should include a problem-solving slot (the single most important habit for JEE), and every week should include one full-length mock test plus a revision slot for the previous week's chapters. These come before "extra study" — they are the plan, not additions to it.

6

Review and adjust every Sunday

Spend 20 minutes each Sunday marking what you finished, what slipped, and why. Move unfinished topics forward, but cap the total so the next week stays realistic. A plan that is adjusted weekly is a plan that survives.

How many hours should you study for JEE?

There is no magic number, and asking "how many hours" too early usually produces either guilt or procrastination. A realistic target for most aspirants with school is 6 to 8 hours of focused study per day on a school day, in blocks of 45 to 90 minutes with real breaks, plus at least 7 hours of sleep. During holidays, many students stretch this to 8 to 10 hours — but only if the hours include practice, not just note-making.

Three rules about hours:

  • Quality beats quantity. Two hours of timed problem solving beats five hours of rereading notes.
  • Consistency beats intensity. A steady 6 hours for six days a week outperforms a heroic 12-hour day followed by burnout.
  • Rest is part of the plan. Plan one lighter day a week. Sleep and short breaks are what make the intense days possible.

Balancing JEE and Class 12 boards

JEE Main and the Class 12 boards share a large part of the Physics, Chemistry and Mathematics syllabus, especially through NCERT textbooks. Treat them as one syllabus rather than two enemies:

  • Learn concepts from the NCERT chapters — that serves both boards and JEE.
  • Use separate slots for JEE-style numericals and previous-year questions, which boards rarely require.
  • In the weeks before board exams, shift the balance toward board-style answer-writing and school practicals, then shift back after.

A single combined timetable removes the classic trap of "I'll study for boards until March, then start JEE" — by March, a JEE aspirant should already be in the mock-and-revision phase.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Starting new topics right up to the exam and never reaching full revision.
  • Taking mocks without analysing mistakes — the mock then teaches you almost nothing.
  • Dropping a subject for weeks at a time to "fix" another subject.
  • Rereading and highlighting instead of solving problems.
  • Copying someone else's 14-hour schedule and quitting after three days.
  • Skipping sleep to study — fatigue destroys the accuracy JEE rewards.

Tools that help you stick to the plan

Planning tools work best when they remove friction: a calendar that blocks your study slots, a syllabus tracker you update daily, and practice sets you can do without hunting for material. An AI study planner can turn this strategy into a structured weekly plan and track your progress, and JEE practice quizzes give you fast, targeted practice with instant feedback on weak topics. None of these replace the plan above — they make it easier to follow.

JEE study plan FAQs

How many hours should a JEE aspirant study per day?

Most serious JEE aspirants aim for 6 to 8 hours of focused study a day outside school, split into shorter blocks of 45 to 90 minutes with breaks. Consistency and practice matter more than the total number of hours. Sleep for at least 7 hours and schedule a weekly rest slot.

How do I make a study timetable for JEE preparation?

Audit your syllabus and deadline, set a target score, divide topics by subject weightage and difficulty, then build a weekly timetable that covers all three subjects every day or every alternate day. Reserve fixed slots for practice problems, revision, and one mock test a week, and review the timetable every Sunday.

Can I prepare for JEE while studying in Class 12 with boards?

Yes. JEE Main syllabus overlaps heavily with the Class 12 NCERT syllabus for Physics, Chemistry and Mathematics. Treat boards and JEE as one syllabus: learn concepts from NCERT, and use separate slots for JEE-level numerical practice and past papers. A single combined timetable reduces the feeling of being pulled in two directions.

How many mock tests should I take for JEE?

Most successful candidates start taking a full mock test once a week about three months before the exam, then move to one every few days in the final month. After every mock, spend at least as much time analysing errors as you spent taking the test. A mock without analysis teaches you very little.

How do I revise for JEE without forgetting topics?

Use spaced repetition: revise a topic the next day, then after 3 days, then weekly. Keep a one-page formula and concept sheet for every chapter and scan it daily. Daily practice of 10 to 15 previously-solved problems also keeps earlier chapters fresh.

What is a realistic JEE preparation timeline?

A common realistic timeline is 12 to 18 months: the first phase focuses on learning the full syllabus thoroughly, the middle phase on practice and error analysis, and the last three months on mocks and revision. With a shorter window, prioritise high-weightage topics and consistent daily practice over perfect coverage.

Key takeaways
  1. Audit the syllabus, set a target based on your mocks, and divide the work into weekly chunks.
  2. Build a timetable with fixed daily slots for each subject, plus protected practice, revision and mock slots.
  3. Take one full mock a week and spend equal time analysing it.
  4. Treat boards and JEE as one syllabus to avoid the March panic.
  5. Review the plan every Sunday and adjust it — a plan you follow for six weeks beats a perfect plan you abandon in three days.
